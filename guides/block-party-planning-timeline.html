Block Party Planning Timeline

A Brooklyn block party timeline that works backwards from the fun: the 60-day permit clock, board follow-up, food notices, and when to book equipment.

Updated October 27, 2026

A block party has one immovable object — the city's permit clock — and a dozen movable parts around it. Plan backwards from party day and the whole thing stays calm. Here's the sequence that works, with the two dates nobody should have to learn twice: the permit application goes in 60 days out, minimum, and equipment gets booked at least 2 weeks out.

The checklist version

  • Permit filed on E-Apply (60 days out, $25 fee)
  • Community board's own deadline checked — and met
  • Precinct's denied-block and blocked-date lists checked
  • Sound permit filed if anything's amplified
  • Food plan set — DOHMH coordinated if the public is eating
  • Spray cap requested from the FDNY if the hydrant's in the plan
  • Equipment booked (2+ weeks out)
  • Rain plan agreed (there's a page for that — the city doesn't do rain dates)
  • Budget collected (the planner helps)
  • Confirmation call done

Notice what's not on the list: anything about games, tables, or the drink station after the 2-week mark. That's deliberate — the equipment is the easy part, and it stays easy right up until the confirmation call makes it certain.

Frequently asked

What is the most common block party planning mistake?

Treating the 60-day permit filing as the only deadline. Community boards can want more notice, food for the public adds a 30-day Health Department track, and precinct requirements vary. File early, then work the checklist backwards from party day.

When should I book rentals for a block party?

At least 2 weeks before your date — BlockParty's minimum lead — but book as soon as your permit filing is in. The permit is the long pole; early booking locks your equipment while it's still available.
